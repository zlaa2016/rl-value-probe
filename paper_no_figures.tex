\section*{Executive Summary}

This pilot asks whether intermediate representations and token-level confidence
correspond to terminal instruction-following reward. I evaluated OLMo 3 Base,
SFT, DPO, and RLVR checkpoints on six prompts with five stochastic rollouts per
prompt and checkpoint, producing 120 responses. IFEvalG supplied
constraint-level terminal rewards; hidden states and confidence were recorded
at four generation fractions.

The central result is a failure of identification, not a successful probe.
Prompt identity explained 79.8--100\% of reward variance within checkpoint,
and only four of 24 checkpoint-by-prompt cells varied across rollouts. Several
pooled trajectory correlations changed sign after prompt centering; RLVR's
75\% activation-change correlation changed from 0.417 to $-0.306$. At 25\%, a
verifier applied directly to partial text already matched terminal reward in
80--93\% of responses, showing how high predictive accuracy can arise without
a latent value estimate. The held-out rewards were all zero, every response
reached the 128-token cap, and the prompt-clustered interval for the
RLVR-minus-Base mean included zero. The experiment therefore does not establish
trajectory-specific reward decodability, causal faithfulness, or
generalization; it identifies the controls and counterfactual variation needed
to test those claims.

\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning for language models commonly assigns reward after a
complete response has been generated. The terminal score indicates whether an
outcome met an external objective, but it does not reveal when information
about that outcome became available inside the model. If intermediate hidden
states contain outcome-relevant information, they may support monitoring of a
generation before the terminal verifier is applied. If inexpensive confidence
statistics contain the same information, they may provide a simpler signal.
Conversely, confidence may rise even when a response violates externally
specified constraints.

This work studies that distinction in a small, controlled instruction-following
setting. IFEval-style prompts are useful because they attach deterministic,
programmatically checkable constraints to natural-language responses
\citep{zhou2023ifeval}. I compare four publicly released checkpoints from the
same OLMo model family, reducing architectural differences relative to a
comparison of unrelated models \citep{groeneveld2024olmo,olmo3card}.

The study asks three questions that are deliberately stricter than whether a
probe can fit reward labels:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Within the same prompt, do stochastic rollouts with different
    terminal outcomes also differ systematically in hidden state or policy
    confidence?
    \item Do internal features contain outcome information beyond prompt
    identity and constraint progress already observable in the partial text?
    \item Are changes across Base, SFT, DPO, and RLVR broad across prompts, or
    concentrated in particular instructions and constraints?
\end{enumerate}

These questions concern \emph{internal--behavioral correspondence}. Even
perfect held-out prediction would establish only that reward is decodable
under a particular data split. It would not show that the model uses the
decoded direction, that the signal estimates future reward rather than
restating visible prefix content, or that it transfers across tasks and model
stages. Evidence for those stronger claims would require incremental prediction
over observable controls, robustness across prompt families, matched-prefix
comparisons, and ultimately intervention. Similarly, the comparison across
checkpoints is descriptive. Although prior work has contrasted memorization
under SFT with generalization under outcome-based RL \citep{chu2025sft}, the
present data do not provide the unseen task variants required to test that
claim.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Internal Feedback and Confidence-Based Reward}

Recent work asks whether a model's own uncertainty can replace or supplement
external supervision in reinforcement learning for reasoning
\citep{zhang2025survey}. Intuitor uses self-certainty as its sole reward,
whereas RENT reinforces high-confidence reasoning trajectories and entropy
minimization directly concentrates probability on confident outputs
\citep{zhao2025intuitor,prabhudesai2025rent,agarwal2025entropy}. These methods
provide evidence that internal uncertainty signals can sometimes support
learning without labeled solutions. However, \citet{zhang2025nofreelunch}
report that gains from internal feedback can diminish or reverse over training
and can be limited for instruction-tuned models.

The present study tests a narrower prerequisite for using confidence as reward:
whether confidence measured during generation corresponds to an independent
terminal verifier. Unlike the cited training studies, I do not optimize an
internal signal. I compare its trajectory post hoc with exact
instruction-following outcomes. This separation is important because confidence
may be useful as an optimization signal in some reasoning domains without being
a calibrated estimate of constraint satisfaction in this setting.

\subsection{External, Internal, and Structured Rewards}

RL for language models can combine several sources of feedback. For example,
Shop-R1 combines an internal signal for rationale generation with structured
rewards for action type and action details in an online-shopping environment
\citep{zhang2025shopr1}. IFEval instead provides deterministic checks for
explicit natural-language instructions \citep{zhou2023ifeval}. The current
experiment uses the latter as an external terminal criterion and retains each
constraint outcome separately. This makes it possible to identify cases in
which a high-confidence response remains behaviorally incorrect under the
specified instruction.

\subsection{Post-Training and Generalization}

\citet{chu2025sft} compare SFT and outcome-based RL on explicitly held-out task
variants and report stronger generalization from RL, while also finding that
SFT supports subsequent RL by stabilizing output format. That result motivates
the cross-checkpoint comparison here, but the experimental claims are not the
same. My OLMo checkpoints are sequential released stages, and my prompts come
from an RLVR training-source distribution. I therefore examine changes in
behavior, confidence, and representation dynamics across stages without
interpreting them as a direct replication of the SFT-versus-RL generalization
result.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Experimental Design and Checkpoints}

The experiment used four OLMo 3 7B checkpoints:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Base}: \texttt{allenai/Olmo-3-1025-7B};
    \item \textbf{SFT}: \texttt{allenai/Olmo-3-7B-Think-SFT};
    \item \textbf{DPO}: \texttt{allenai/Olmo-3-7B-Think-DPO}; and
    \item \textbf{RLVR}: \texttt{allenai/Olmo-3-7B-Think}.
\end{itemize}

For every checkpoint, the model generated five stochastic rollouts for each of
six prompts. Generation used temperature 0.6, top-$p$ 0.95, random seed 0,
and a maximum of 128 new tokens. The same prompt IDs and generation settings
were used for all checkpoints. The resulting dataset contains

\begin{equation}
4\ \text{checkpoints}\times 6\ \text{prompts}\times
5\ \text{rollouts}=120\ \text{responses}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Prompt Source}

Prompts were streamed from revision
\texttt{0fb6466d31ef3a9dd16985ef635e6429e05a6491} of
\texttt{allenai/Dolci-Think-RL-7B}. The loader selected examples whose
\texttt{original\_dataset} field was
\texttt{hamishivi/IF\_multi\_constraints\_upto5\_filtered}. This source was
part of the data used for OLMo 3 RLVR training \citep{dolcirl}. Consequently,
the experiment measures behavior on an RLVR training-source distribution, not
on a held-out task distribution.

\subsection{Terminal Reward}

Each completed response was evaluated with the IFEvalG instruction registry
from Ai2's \texttt{open-instruct} implementation \citep{openinstruct}. If a
prompt specifies $m$ constraints, terminal reward is

\begin{equation}
R_T=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}
\mathbb{I}[\text{constraint}_j\ \text{is satisfied}].
\end{equation}

The analysis retained both $R_T$ and the binary result for every individual
constraint. Reward therefore measures the fraction of requested formatting or
lexical constraints satisfied; it is not a general measure of factual quality.

\subsection{Trajectory Measurements}

After generation, the prompt and completed response were passed through the
same checkpoint again with hidden-state output enabled. Measurements were
taken at the generated token nearest each trajectory fraction

\begin{equation}
t/T\in\{0.25,0.50,0.75,1.00\}.
\end{equation}

Residual-stream vectors were retained from transformer layers
$\ell\in\{8,16,24,32\}$. Each saved activation is a single-token vector, not a
mean-pooled response representation. For descriptive plots, final-layer
movement relative to the 25\% position was measured as

\begin{equation}
D_t=\frac{\lVert h^{(32)}_t-h^{(32)}_{0.25T}\rVert_2}{\sqrt{d}}.
\end{equation}

Thus $D_{0.25T}=0$ by construction. For predictive analysis, the implementation
also formed adjacent differences $\Delta h_t=h_t-h_{t-1}$.

For every generated token, the implementation computed chosen-token log
probability, predictive entropy, and top-1 next-token probability. Prefix means
and last-token values were saved at each trajectory fraction. Descriptive
confidence was the geometric mean sampled-token probability

\begin{equation}
C_t=\exp\left(\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}
\log p_\theta(y_i\mid x,y_{<i})\right).
\end{equation}

This is a policy sharpness measure, not a calibrated probability that the
response will satisfy the verifier.

\subsection{Descriptive Analyses}

For each checkpoint and trajectory fraction, Pearson and Spearman correlations
were computed for $C_t$ versus $R_T$, $D_t$ versus $R_T$, and $C_t$ versus
$D_t$. Constraint pass rates were calculated separately by verifier instruction
ID and checkpoint. These statistics pool the 30 rollouts from each checkpoint;
because five rollouts share each prompt, they are treated as descriptive rather
than as independent inferential observations.

\subsection{Evidential Audit}

The initial pooled analysis can confound two questions: whether some prompts
are generally easier than others, and whether the model's evolving state
distinguishes successful from unsuccessful rollouts of the \emph{same} prompt.
I therefore performed four post-hoc identification checks that do not fit an
additional activation probe.

First, I decomposed reward variation into between-prompt and within-prompt
components for each checkpoint. The between-prompt share is the in-sample
$R^2$ obtained by predicting every rollout with its prompt's mean reward. It is
therefore a prompt-identity baseline, not an internal-state model. Second, I
recomputed each trajectory correlation after subtracting the corresponding
prompt mean from both the trajectory variable and reward. This fixed-effect
correlation asks whether the association survives removal of average prompt
difficulty. I also recorded its range under leave-one-prompt-out sensitivity
analysis.

Third, I applied the same external constraint verifier to the saved partial
text at each trajectory fraction. This diagnostic measures how much apparent
early predictability is available directly from observable constraint progress,
without activations. It is not treated as a valid early reward by itself:
several constraints are non-monotonic, and agreement can be inflated when both
prefix and terminal scores are zero.

Fourth, checkpoint differences were computed at the prompt level rather than
treating 30 rollouts as independent. For each post-trained checkpoint I report
the six paired prompt differences from Base, a prompt-clustered percentile
bootstrap interval, and an exact paired sign-flip test. With only six prompt
clusters these quantities are sensitivity summaries, not precise population
estimates.

\subsection{Held-Out Reward Probes}

Ridge-regression probes were fit separately for each checkpoint, layer, and
trajectory fraction. The implemented feature sets were:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item current activation $h_t$;
    \item concatenated activation history $[h_{0.25T};\ldots;h_t]$;
    \item adjacent activation change $\Delta h_t$;
    \item six policy-confidence statistics; and
    \item current activation combined with confidence statistics.
\end{enumerate}

The Base checkpoint served as the model baseline. No TF--IDF baseline was used.
One fixed 75/25 split was grouped by prompt, placing four prompt groups in the
training set and two in the test set. All five rollouts from a prompt remained
in the same split. The statistical null permuted complete prompt-level blocks
of training rewards before refitting each probe. Recorded metrics included
held-out $R^2$, mean squared error, rank correlation, and the prompt-block
permutation distribution. Each checkpoint-specific probe nevertheless had only
20 training responses, compared with 4,096 current-state features and as many
as 16,384 activation-history features. Regularization makes estimation
possible, but does not make training fit or a single favorable split
scientifically diagnostic. Probe evidence was therefore required to show
non-degenerate held-out discrimination and improvement over observable
controls; the completed data did not meet those requirements.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Terminal Reward Varied More by Prompt Than by Checkpoint}

Table~\ref{tab:reward-summary} reports aggregate verifier reward. Mean reward
rose from 0.1017 for Base to 0.1372 for RLVR, an absolute difference of 0.0355.
The increase was not broad across prompts. Three prompts received reward zero
for every checkpoint, and one prompt received exactly 0.25 for every
checkpoint. Prompt 639-88 improved from 0.0000 for Base to 0.3333 for DPO and
RLVR, whereas prompt 721-31 decreased from 0.3600 for Base to 0.2400 for RLVR.

At the prompt level, the RLVR-minus-Base differences were positive for one
prompt, negative for one, and exactly zero for four. The mean paired difference
was 0.0356, but its prompt-clustered bootstrap interval was
$[-0.060,0.167]$ and the exact paired sign-flip $p$-value was 1.0. SFT and DPO
showed the same one-positive, one-negative, four-unchanged pattern, with
intervals that also included zero. These summaries do not demonstrate a
checkpoint-level improvement; they show that the aggregate ordering can be
produced by two influential prompts.

All 120 generations contained exactly 128 generated tokens. The maximum-token
limit therefore censored every response, including responses evaluated with
completion-sensitive constraints. Strictly, the recorded $R_T$ is a verifier
score at an imposed cutoff, not reward at a model-selected terminal state. This
distinction is especially consequential for final-word, exact-length, and
complete-structure requirements.

\subsection{Reward Was Nearly Determined by Prompt Identity}

Only four of the 24 checkpoint-by-prompt cells contained more than one reward
value across their five stochastic rollouts. DPO had no within-prompt reward
variation at all; Base and RLVR each had one varying prompt, and SFT had two.
Predicting each rollout with its prompt's mean reward accounted for 95.3\% of
reward variance for Base, 79.8\% for SFT, 100\% for DPO, and 94.9\% for RLVR.

This result changes the interpretation of any high probe score. Hidden states
are functions of the prompt and can encode prompt identity, while the activation
vector has far more dimensions than there are prompt groups. Consequently, a
probe may recover nearly all reward variance by identifying which prompt
produced the activation, without representing rollout-specific future success.
Repeated generations
increase the number of rows from six to 30 per checkpoint, but when their
outcomes are constant within prompt they do not create the counterfactual
variation required to distinguish prompt recognition from value estimation.

\subsection{Constraint Outcomes Were Sparse and Highly Specific}

The observed constraint pass rates were sparse and strongly dependent on
constraint identity.
The exclude-word and palindrome constraints were passed in every
rollout for every checkpoint. In contrast, JSON formatting, first-word,
same-start/end, required-last-word, paragraph-first-word, exact-word-count, and
counting-composition constraints were never passed in this sample.


The clearest post-training increase occurred for the requirement that a
specified word appear exactly once: its pass rate was 0\% for Base, 50\% for
SFT, 50\% for DPO, and 60\% for RLVR. The lowercase requirement showed the
opposite pattern, with 80\% for Base and 0\% for all three post-trained
checkpoints. Because constraint identity is nearly confounded with prompt
identity in this sample, these values describe the six selected prompts rather
than general constraint-level capabilities.

\subsection{Pooled Trajectory Correlations Did Not Survive Identification Checks}

Mean generated-token confidence increased across the Base trajectory, from
0.626 at 25\% to 0.698 at completion. In contrast, mean confidence decreased
over generation for SFT (0.821 to 0.736), DPO (0.890 to 0.853), and RLVR
(0.865 to 0.817). Post-trained checkpoints nevertheless remained more
confident on average than Base at every sampled fraction.

Final activation-change RMS was 1.473 for Base, 1.776 for SFT, 1.803 for DPO,
and 1.809 for RLVR. These values indicate larger movement away from the 25\%
state in the post-trained checkpoints, but movement magnitude alone does not
identify a reward-relevant direction.

The Pearson correlations with terminal reward changed substantially over the
trajectory.
For SFT, DPO, and RLVR, confidence--reward correlation began
positive at 25\% (0.560, 0.586, and 0.557 respectively) and fell during the
rollout. At completion, the correlations were $-0.110$, $-0.261$, and
$-0.374$. Base remained weakly positive, ending at 0.210.


Activation-change correlation was not consistent across checkpoints. DPO rose
from 0.185 at 50\% to 0.390 at completion. RLVR rose from 0.271 at 50\% to
0.417 at 75\%, then fell to 0.052 at completion. SFT and Base ended at 0.134
and 0.188.

These pooled coefficients do not isolate trajectory-specific information. At
25\%, SFT confidence had a pooled correlation of 0.560 but a prompt-centered
correlation of $-0.185$. At 75\%, RLVR activation change had a pooled
correlation of 0.417 but a prompt-centered correlation of $-0.306$. DPO's
within-prompt correlations were undefined at every fraction because each
prompt had a constant reward across its five rollouts. Leave-one-prompt-out
ranges were also wide: for example, Base confidence at 25\% ranged from
$-0.116$ to 0.483, and SFT confidence at completion ranged from $-0.466$ to
0.068. Thus the apparent trajectory relationships are sensitive to prompt
composition and, in several cases, reverse after prompt adjustment. They do not
provide stable evidence that confidence or activation movement tracks future
reward within a rollout.

\subsection{Observable Prefix Status Was a Strong Competing Explanation}

The external verifier was applied to each saved partial response as a diagnostic
of information already visible in the text. At 25\% of generation, the prefix
score exactly equaled the eventual terminal score for 93.3\% of Base responses,
83.3\% of SFT responses, 83.3\% of DPO responses, and 80.0\% of RLVR responses.
The corresponding pooled correlations with terminal reward were 0.964, 0.592,
0.500, and 0.527. By 50\%, exact agreement ranged from 90.0\% to 96.7\%.

These large values should not be interpreted as successful early prediction.
They are inflated by prompt-specific constraints and by the many cases in which
both prefix and terminal reward are zero. Instead, they demonstrate the main
identification problem: a predictor can achieve impressive agreement without
recovering a latent belief about future success. A meaningful activation result
would have to improve on this observable-prefix information on prompts with
genuine within-prompt outcome variation. The current held-out split cannot
perform that comparison.

\subsection{Held-Out Reward Decodability Was Not Evaluable}

The fixed split assigned prompts 335-54 and 935-9 to the test set. Both prompts
received terminal reward zero in all five rollouts from all four checkpoints.
Consequently, test-target variance was zero for every probe evaluation.
Held-out $R^2$ and rank correlation were therefore undefined across all 256
checkpoint, layer, fraction, and feature-set combinations.

Mean squared error can still be calculated against a constant target, but it
does not measure discrimination between successful and unsuccessful held-out
outcomes. The shuffled-label null likewise cannot repair the missing outcome
variation. Accordingly, this experiment yields no evidence for or against
linear terminal-reward decodability. It instead identifies a data-coverage
failure in the pilot evaluation.

\section{Discussion}

The experiment does not currently support its central hypothesis that
trajectory-specific internal state provides a useful estimate of future
terminal reward. This is not because a probe obtained a low score. It is
because the data do not separate the proposed signal from simpler
explanations. Prompt identity almost determines reward, partial text exposes
much of the same structure, the held-out target is constant, and only a handful
of prompt-by-checkpoint cells contain different outcomes across rollouts.

This distinction is essential for interpreting probes. A 100\% training score
would be unsurprising in a setting with 20 training responses, 4,096--16,384
activation features, and labels that are nearly deterministic functions of six
prompt identities. Such a score would show that the representation contains
enough information to reconstruct the dataset labels. It would not show that the
model maintains a value estimate, that the decoded feature precedes the
outcome, or that the model uses it to select behavior. Even a high held-out
score can remain ambiguous if the split preserves the same constraint
templates or if an observable prefix baseline performs equally well.

The Base checkpoint is an experimental reference, not a sufficient statistical
control for this inference. A stronger Base-to-RLVR probe does not by itself
show that RL created or amplified a reward representation: RL may alter prompt
encoding, residual scale, generated text, or separability without improving a
model-internal estimate of future reward. Direct comparison of RMS distances
across checkpoints is especially limited because the checkpoints inhabit
different representation spaces and generate different token sequences.

The confidence results are therefore narrower than a claim that confidence is
anti-faithful. Post-trained models were more confident on average, and their
pooled confidence--reward association became negative late in generation. But
prompt centering changed several coefficients substantially or reversed their
sign. The supported conclusion is that confidence was not a stable surrogate
for IFEvalG reward in this sample. The study does not establish a general
negative relationship between confidence and instruction following.

Likewise, the aggregate reward ordering is not evidence that post-training
improved instruction following generally. Four prompts were unchanged between
Base and RLVR, one improved, and one worsened. All responses were length
censored, and the prompts came from an RLVR training-source distribution. The
comparison therefore cannot distinguish memorization, elicitation, or
generalization.

\subsection{What Would Constitute Stronger Evidence}

A decisive follow-up should make the unit of analysis a prefix state rather
than a completed response. For each saved prefix, multiple independent suffixes
should be sampled from the \emph{same} prefix, and their mean verifier outcome
should estimate the continuation value

\begin{equation}
V(h_t) = \mathbb{E}[R_T\mid x,y_{\leq t}].
\end{equation}

The dataset should contain many such prefixes with non-degenerate continuation
outcomes, drawn from held-out prompt and constraint families. An activation
probe would then be informative only if it predicted this estimated value on
held-out families, improved over policy confidence and current partial-verifier
status, remained stable across resampling and model stages, and beat a
prompt-block permutation null. To isolate weight changes, all checkpoints
should also be evaluated on identical teacher-forced prefixes before comparing
their representations. Finally, intervention along a reproducible reward
direction followed by resampling would be needed to move from decodability to a
causal claim. These tests address value representation, transfer, and causal
use separately instead of collapsing them into one probe metric.

\section{Limitations}

\paragraph{Small number of prompt groups.}
The 120 responses contain only six distinct prompts. Repeated rollouts estimate
sampling variation for those prompts but do not substitute for broader prompt
coverage. Prompt identity is also strongly associated with both reward and
constraint identity.

\paragraph{Insufficient within-prompt outcome variation.}
Twenty of the 24 checkpoint-by-prompt cells have constant reward across all
five rollouts. The nominal sample size of 120 therefore overstates the amount
of information available for distinguishing trajectory success from prompt
difficulty.

\paragraph{High-dimensional, low-sample probes.}
Each checkpoint-specific split provides 20 training responses. Current-state
features have 4,096 dimensions, and activation histories have up to 16,384.
Ridge regularization limits coefficient magnitude but cannot make in-sample fit
or one split evidence of a general reward representation.

\paragraph{Universal length censoring.}
Every response reached the 128-token maximum. The measured reward therefore
combines instruction-following performance with truncation effects and should
be interpreted as cutoff reward rather than reward on a naturally completed
trajectory.

\paragraph{Degenerate held-out target.}
Both test prompts had constant zero reward. The central held-out probe analysis
could not measure reward discrimination, and no conclusion about reward
decodability follows from this run.

\paragraph{Training-source evaluation.}
The sampled prompts came from the dataset used for RLVR training. The results do
not measure model generalization to unseen examples or task variants.

\paragraph{Checkpoint and trajectory confounding.}
Each checkpoint generated its own continuation. Cross-checkpoint activation
differences may therefore reflect both changed weights and changed tokens.

\paragraph{Coarse, single-token activation sampling.}
Only four positions and four layers were retained. Each activation represents
one token position rather than a pooled or token-complete trajectory, so brief
or distributed signals may be missed. The 100\% state is observed only after
the final generated token and is therefore a post-outcome representation, not
an early prediction. Four observations per response also do not support a
well-identified time-series model of representation dynamics.

\paragraph{Observable-prefix confounding.}
Activations can encode prompt identity and surface features of the generated
prefix. The post-hoc partial-verifier audit demonstrates that much of the
terminal label is already predictable from those observables, but the current
split cannot estimate whether activations add information beyond them.

\paragraph{Observational analysis.}
Correlations and probes test association and decodability. No activation
intervention was performed, so the experiment does not establish causal
faithfulness.

\paragraph{Narrow external objective.}
IFEvalG reward measures explicit verifiable constraints. It does not measure
all aspects of factual correctness, usefulness, or human preference. It also
normalizes by a prompt-specific number of heterogeneous constraints, so equal
scalar rewards across prompts need not represent the same behavior.

\section{Conclusion}

The completed pilot does not show that an LLM's intermediate trajectory
contains a general, trajectory-specific representation of terminal
instruction-following reward. The strongest empirical fact is that reward was
nearly determined by prompt identity: a prompt-mean predictor explained
79.8--100\% of variance within checkpoint, while most repeated-rollout cells
had no outcome variation. Pooled activation and confidence correlations were
therefore vulnerable to prompt confounding, and the held-out probe had no
reward variance with which to test discrimination. Partial text also reproduced
the terminal score at high rates, showing why predictive accuracy alone would
not identify an internal value signal.

The defensible conclusion is methodological. Probing is useful only after the
experiment creates counterfactual variation in future outcomes from comparable
prefix states and tests incremental information beyond observable behavior.
Cross-prompt prediction, cross-checkpoint comparison, and causal intervention
answer additional questions and should not be inferred from one another. The
current pipeline supplies the required measurements, but the next experiment
must redesign the sampling unit around branched continuations and balanced,
held-out constraints before claims about value representation, RL-induced
generalization, or faithfulness can be evaluated.

\section{Implementation Summary}

The implementation contains the following components:

\begin{itemize}
    \item loading matched OLMo 3 Base, SFT, DPO, and RLVR checkpoints;
    \item streaming instruction-following prompts from a pinned revision of
    \texttt{Dolci-Think-RL-7B};
    \item sampling five stochastic rollouts per prompt and checkpoint;
    \item computing aggregate and individual IFEvalG constraint outcomes;
    \item extracting residual-stream states from four transformer layers at
    four trajectory fractions;
    \item computing prefix and last-token log probability, entropy, and top-1
    probability;
    \item fitting current-state, activation-history, activation-difference,
    confidence, and activation-plus-confidence Ridge probes;
    \item using a prompt-grouped fixed holdout and prompt-block shuffled-label
    null;
    \item auditing prompt-level variance, prompt-centered correlations,
    leave-one-prompt-out sensitivity, and partial-text verifier status; and
    \item saving rollout, activation, prediction, correlation, plot, and W\&B
    artifact outputs.
\end{itemize}

The Base checkpoint is the model baseline. No TF--IDF history baseline or
POMDP simulation is part of the reported experiment.

\section{Example Prompt and Checkpoint Outcomes}

Prompt 639-88 asked the model to explain human digestion while satisfying three
verifiable constraints: paragraph 3 had to begin with \emph{art}, the keyword
\emph{being} had to appear exactly once, and the response had to end with
\emph{stroke}.

\begin{quote}
\small
``Can you list, explain, and give every individual reaction involved in the
process of human digestion from ingestion to excretion? There should be 4
paragraphs. Paragraphs and only paragraphs are separated with each other by two
new lines as if it was \texttt{'\textbackslash n\textbackslash n'} in python.
Paragraph 3 must start with word \emph{art}. Include keyword \emph{being} in
your response. The last word of your response should be the word
\emph{stroke}.''
\end{quote}
